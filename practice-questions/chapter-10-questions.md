## Chapter 10: Client Discovery, Scoping, and Business Case Development — Practice Questions

**Q1.** During discovery, you identify a VP of Engineering who has high influence over the technical decision but low day-to-day interest in the project, and a Data Analyst who has high interest but almost no formal authority. Using the power/interest stakeholder mapping grid, how should you engage each?

A) Manage the VP closely with frequent detailed updates; monitor the Analyst with minimal contact
B) Ignore the VP since they are not interested; manage the Analyst closely since they will use the solution daily
C) Treat both identically since stakeholder mapping only applies to executives
D) Keep the VP satisfied with periodic high-level updates; keep the Analyst informed with regular detailed communication

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

High power/low interest = 'Keep Satisfied' (periodic, high-level updates that respect their limited time). Low power/high interest = 'Keep Informed' (regular, detailed updates since they're engaged but can't unilaterally decide).

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Reverses the quadrant strategy — 'Manage Closely' is reserved for high power AND high interest stakeholders, not just high power
- Ignoring a high-power stakeholder risks losing executive buy-in even if they seem disengaged
- The power/interest grid exists specifically because engagement strategy must differ by quadrant

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q2.** A client's CFO asks for the projected ROI before approving budget for a 12-month automation project. Implementation cost is $240,000, and the solution eliminates $30,000/month in manual labor costs. What is the approximate payback period?

A) 4 months
B) 8 months
C) 12 months
D) 24 months

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

$240,000 / $30,000 per month = 8 months payback period.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- 4 months would require $60,000/month in savings, which is double the stated figure
- 12 months is the project duration, not the payback period — a common conflation
- 24 months would apply if savings were only $10,000/month

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q3.** You are drafting an executive summary for a business case aimed at a non-technical CFO. Which approach best follows technical writing principles for non-technical audiences?

A) Lead with detailed architecture diagrams and API specifications to establish technical credibility
B) Lead with the business outcome and cost impact in the first two sentences, then support with minimal technical detail as needed
C) Use the same content as the technical design document but shorten it by half
D) Avoid all numbers since executives prefer narrative-driven storytelling

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Executive audiences need the 'so what' immediately — outcome and cost/benefit up front — with technical detail subordinated and simplified.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Architecture diagrams and API specs bury the business value the CFO actually cares about
- Shortening a technical document doesn't change its audience-inappropriate framing or jargon
- Executives typically want concrete numbers (cost, ROI, timeline) — removing them weakens the business case

</details>

**Q4.** Which discovery methodology is BEST suited when the primary goal is qualifying whether a deal has an identified economic buyer, clear decision criteria, and a champion inside the account?

A) SPIN Selling
B) BANT
C) SWOT Analysis
D) MEDDIC

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

MEDDIC explicitly covers Metrics, Economic buyer, Decision criteria, Decision process, Identify pain, and Champion — matching the scenario's focus.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- SPIN focuses on Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff questioning, not buyer/decision structure qualification
- BANT covers Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline — simpler and lacks explicit champion/decision-criteria qualification
- SWOT is a strategic planning framework, not a sales/discovery qualification methodology

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q5.** You are structuring a 2-hour solution scoping workshop with both business stakeholders and technical leads present. Which agenda structure best balances both audiences?

A) 90 minutes of deep technical architecture review, followed by 30 minutes for business questions
B) Skip business context entirely since technical leads already understand the requirements
C) Open with business goals and success criteria (30 min), move to current-state/pain points (30 min), then future-state solution options with trade-offs (45 min), close with next steps and owners (15 min)
D) Alternate strictly every 10 minutes between business and technical topics regardless of natural flow

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

This structure grounds the workshop in business context first (why we're here), diagnoses the current state, explores solution trade-offs for both audiences, and ends with concrete accountability — a standard scoping workshop flow.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Front-loading deep technical content alienates business stakeholders and risks losing their engagement before their goals are addressed
- Business stakeholders often hold budget authority and success criteria that technical leads don't fully know
- Rigid time-boxing ignores natural discussion flow and can cut off critical trade-off conversations

</details>

**Q6.** A technical architect wants to present a proposed solution to three audiences in one meeting: the CTO (technical depth), the VP of Operations (business impact), and the CFO (cost/ROI). What is the BEST approach for this cross-layer communication?

A) Present one generic slide deck covering all three areas equally in sequence, regardless of who is speaking
B) Structure the presentation with a shared business outcome framing up front, then layer in technical depth, operational impact, and cost detail as distinct sections each audience can tune into
C) Only address the CFO's concerns since budget approval is the biggest blocker
D) Send three separate technical documents in advance and skip the joint discussion

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Effective cross-layer communication anchors on a shared outcome, then provides audience-specific sections so each stakeholder gets relevant depth without forcing others to sit through irrelevant detail.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- A single undifferentiated deck fails to address the distinct concerns of each audience layer
- Ignoring the CTO and VP risks losing technical and operational buy-in even if budget is approved
- Skipping the joint discussion removes the opportunity for stakeholders to align in real time, which is often the workshop's main value

</details>

**Q7.** In the power/interest stakeholder mapping grid, which quadrant requires the LEAST proactive engagement effort?

A) High power, high interest
B) High power, low interest
C) Low power, low interest
D) Low power, high interest

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

Low power/low interest stakeholders fall into the 'Monitor' quadrant, requiring minimal effort — just periodic awareness checks.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- High power/high interest requires the most engagement ('Manage Closely')
- High power/low interest still requires periodic satisfaction efforts due to their influence
- Low power/high interest requires ongoing informational updates to keep them engaged

</details>

**Q8.** A client's business case includes hard savings of $150,000/year in labor costs and soft benefits like improved employee morale and faster decision-making. How should soft benefits be handled when articulating ROI to an executive sponsor?

A) Exclude soft benefits entirely since they cannot be quantified and would weaken the business case
B) Include soft benefits qualitatively alongside the quantified hard ROI, clearly labeling them as supporting rather than primary financial justification
C) Convert soft benefits into arbitrary dollar figures and add them directly to the hard ROI number to inflate the total
D) Present only soft benefits since executives respond better to narrative than numbers

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Best practice separates quantified hard ROI from qualitative soft benefits, presenting both but not conflating them — this preserves credibility while still capturing the full value story.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Excluding soft benefits loses valuable context that can strengthen stakeholder buy-in, especially for change-management-sensitive projects
- Fabricating dollar values for unquantifiable benefits undermines the credibility of the entire business case if challenged
- Executives evaluating budget decisions still require quantified financial justification, not narrative alone

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>
